The Data

Evidence of the Value Gap

What the research and one worker’s testimony show about where value goes, in physical and digital supply chains.

The Smile Curve in Manufacturing

The Smile Curve is a measurable trend that shows how value has shifted away from actual production. Baldwin and Ito (2021) analyzed international databases in the Canadian Journal of Economics to show that the real money has moved toward the ends of the chain.

  • The high ends: research, development, and marketing capture the most profit.
  • The squeezed middle: the physical act of making a product has seen its value plummet.

This shows that our economy values the front end of business far more than the behind-the-scenes physical labor required to make a product exist.

Testimony: Maya’s Story

While the Smile Curve is an economic abstraction, its consequences are felt by millions of real individuals. Maya Aktar, a garment worker originally from Bangladesh, describes the reality of being the “invisible” part of the chain:

“I left my home… for a job at a garment factory in Jordan… we could hardly make ends meet.” (Aktar, 2022)

Maya’s experience as a liaison officer highlights a critical structural barrier: language. She notes that most workers from Bangladesh or India do not speak Arabic or English, and when “instructions, announcements, and financial documents are in these languages,” it creates a wall between workers and their rights (Aktar, 2022). This testimony shows that the “Invisible Chain” is maintained not just by low wages, but by a lack of transparency and communication.

The Digital Poverty Chain: AI Labor

Even in the digital age, the labor of training AI models follows the same patterns of geographical exploitation found in 20th-century manufacturing.

Even in high-tech industries, these patterns remain the same. Casilli et al. (2025) argue that AI production looks much like traditional poverty chains: it relies on an invisible workforce in low-income countries doing repetitive data labeling for very low pay. Their four-country study of data work shows that even when the supply chain changes from physical goods to digital ones, the patterns of exploitation persist. The Smile Curve applies to the digital world just as much as the physical one.

According to the World Development Report 2020, global value chains (GVCs) have shifted from trading finished goods to trading “tasks.” In the AI industry, the task is data labeling. Companies in the Global North outsource the tedious, repetitive work of training AI models to workers in low-income regions. This mirrors the garment industry’s Smile Curve: the high-value coding happens in Silicon Valley, while the essential, labor-intensive labeling happens in the “valley” of the curve for pennies (World Bank, 2020).
