A research project on global supply chains

The Invisible Human Cost of Global Logistics

Why the people who physically make and move our goods are paid the least, and how that gap is built into the system.

Central Question

Why has physical labor become systematically undervalued in a world of instant delivery, and how does this create poverty chains that keep workers in cycles of inequality?

Beyond the Surface: The Complexity of the Chain

Most people view global labor as a simple debate between providing jobs and exploiting workers. My research shows that the problem is a structural design rather than a series of accidents. According to the research of Costinot, Vogel, and Wang (2012) in the American Economic Review, pay gaps are a built-in part of the system’s logic. Because production must happen in a specific order, the most productive workers are pushed to the final stages of the chain. This forces lower-skilled workers in the Global South into the earliest, lowest-paying roles. This efficiency requires a bottom tier of labor to function, meaning the inequality is a feature of the market rather than a bug.

My Stance

I argue that the systematic undervaluation of physical labor is not a market glitch, but a deliberate structural feature of global supply chains. By utilizing the Smile Curve framework, we can see how value is redirected away from manufacturing and toward Western branding, effectively institutionalizing poverty for workers in the Global South.

About the Author

My fascination with global logistics began with a simple observation of the disconnect between the digital ease of “one-click” ordering and the physical reality of the hands that move those products. As a student of Supply Chain and Business Analytics, I began to see that the “invisible” nature of these workers isn’t an accident; it is a requirement for the system’s efficiency. This project is an attempt to map the “Invisible Chain” and understand why the people most essential to our global economy are often the ones the system treats as most disposable.
