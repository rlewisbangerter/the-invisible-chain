Conclusion

Final Reflections: Breaking the Chain

What this research changed in how I see global trade, and what comes next.

Synthesis of Learning

This research process has fundamentally shifted my perspective on global trade. At the start of this project, I viewed the “one-click” convenience of modern logistics as a triumph of technology. However, by applying the Smile Curve framework, I have learned that this efficiency is often built on the deliberate invisibility of labor. Engaging with the testimony of Maya Aktar (2022) was a turning point; it transformed abstract economic “tasks” into the reality of human workers struggling against language barriers and subcontracting loopholes.

I now understand that the “Invisible Chain” isn’t a market failure; it’s a design choice. The high-value branding of the Global North depends on the low-value manufacturing “valley” of the Global South. While AI capital offers some wage prospects for job applicants (Drydakis, 2024), it also risks reinforcing these existing inequalities if not met with radical transparency.

Unresolved Questions & Future Action

Can a consumer truly act ethically in a system designed to hide the worker?

Even with “Fair Trade” labels, the layers of Tier 2 and Tier 3 shadow factories remain largely opaque to the average buyer.

Moving forward, I intend to advocate for Joint-Liability legislation, which holds parent corporations legally responsible for every link in their supply chain. For those looking to take action, I recommend exploring these organizations working to make the invisible chain visible:

  • International Labour Organization (ILO): updates on international labor standards and worker rights.
  • Better Work Jordan: how specific programs are improving the garment sector where Maya Aktar works.
  • Fashion Revolution: a global movement calling for greater transparency in the fashion supply chain.
