The Background

The Logistics of Inequality

How the Smile Curve explains who captures value along a supply chain, and who doesn’t.

Defining the “Smile Curve”

To understand why global labor is so unevenly compensated, we must look at the “Smile Curve.” First proposed in the 1990s, this economic model illustrates how value is distributed across the life of a product.

According to Rungi and Del Prete (2018), the “smile” represents a graph where the two ends, pre-production (R&D and design) and post-production (marketing and retail), command the highest profits. The middle of the curve, which represents the actual physical manufacturing, is a “valley” of low value added. For a non-expert, this explains the “Invisible Chain”: the system is designed to pay the people who physically make the goods the least amount of money.

Key Terms for the Non-Expert

Global North vs. Global South

These terms don’t just refer to geography, but to economic power. The Global North (e.g., the US and EU) typically controls the high-value ends of the Smile Curve, while the Global South (e.g., Bangladesh, Vietnam) is relegated to the low-value manufacturing center.

Shadow Factories

Unregistered manufacturing sites that operate outside government oversight. As noted in the World Development Report 2020, these factories are where the most significant labor rights violations occur, hidden behind layers of subcontracting.

Poverty Chains

Supply chains that rely on the “superexploitation” of workers to remain competitive in a global market.

The History of the “One-Click” World

The modern “Invisible Chain” was powered by the rapid expansion of international trade after 1990. As the World Development Report 2020 explains, countries no longer just trade finished goods; they trade tasks (World Bank, 2020).

A single iPhone or pair of sneakers might cross a dozen borders before reaching your doorstep. This fragmentation of labor allows major brands to distance themselves from the working conditions in the factories they hire. By “unbundling” the production process, corporations have made the worker invisible to the consumer, focusing our attention only on the brand and the price tag.
